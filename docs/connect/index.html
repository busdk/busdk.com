Bus Connect

Put a price on the first message.

Bus Connect is a small, self-hostable messaging daemon. Someone you have no prior relationship with — a person, or an AI agent acting for one — pays a small fee over the x402 HTTP payment protocol to open exactly one message thread with you. After that, the conversation is free and bounded.

No prior relationship is not the same as anonymous. Charging for the first message is a sale, so a real deployment has to know who its customer is well enough to tax, invoice, and record the transaction correctly.

Development status

Bus Connect is an accepted, installed proof of concept, not a released product. The current build binds loopback only and settles against a local fake facilitator, so you can run the complete protocol end to end on your own machine today, but you cannot yet expose it publicly or take real money with it. The tutorial is explicit about that line and about what remains before hosted paid operation.

What problem it addresses

Open inboxes are free to send to, so they fill with things nobody chose to receive. Closed inboxes are unreachable, so genuine first contact from someone you have no prior relationship with becomes impossible. Bus Connect takes a third position: reachable without an introduction, but the first message costs the sender something real.

The fee is friction and revenue, not a spam filter and not a guarantee. It is a non-refundable initiation fee. It buys delivery and consideration of one message. It does not promise a reply, and closing a thread for abuse does not refund it. The product says this in its own advertised terms, so a paying counterparty is told exactly what it is buying before it pays.

How it works

An unpaid request to the public endpoint receives an HTTP 402 challenge describing the exact payment required: protocol version, scheme, network, asset contract, decimals, atomic amount, and destination. A capable client retries with a signed payment authorization. On successful settlement the service creates one thread, stores the paid message, and issues one bearer capability scoped to that thread alone.

The sender then polls and follows up for free within frozen limits, and the operator reads and replies. One signed authorization creates at most one thread, no matter how many times it is presented.

What makes it dependable

Taking money over HTTP means the interesting failure is not a crash, it is a crash at the wrong moment. If settlement succeeds and the response is lost, a naive service either loses a paid thread or creates a second one on retry. Bus Connect commits the intent to settle before contacting the facilitator, so an interrupted payment is a durable, reconcilable record rather than an unknown. An exact replay of the same signed payment and body reconciles into the original thread and re-derives the same capability.

That path is not an aspiration in the documentation. It is exercised by killing the daemon mid-settlement, restarting it, and requiring it to converge on the original conversation with exactly one message.

Storage and boundaries

Bus Connect stores conversations in Bus Events. It requires four behaviors of that store — atomic conditional append, deterministic ordered replay, persistence across a restart, and isolation by authenticated identity — and names no database vendor. It does not select, run, migrate, or back up a database of its own.

A service instance holds all its state in one identity-scoped aggregate, so limits that span conversations, such as how many threads may be open at once, are enforced atomically rather than approximately.

The daemon never holds an outbound payer signer. It receives payments; it cannot send them. Capabilities are never stored in plaintext, never appear in a URL, and never reach a log. Only a keyed digest is persisted, so disclosing the store does not disclose a working capability.

Who runs it

One running instance serves exactly one recipient and one payment destination. There is no registry, no second tenant, and no subscription. The intent is that anyone who wants their own paid endpoint self-hosts the software and becomes their own service provider, keeping their own fees and their own responsibility. Licensing terms for that are covered on the contact page.

Knowing who your customer is

The x402 exchange establishes that a payment was authorized and settled. It establishes nothing else. A wallet address is not a name, a country, a tax status, or a contact address, and an operator who has only that cannot issue a receipt, decide the right VAT treatment, or keep the records a sale requires.

For a Finnish company selling a digital service into the EU, that gap is not a detail to add later. The initiation flow has to establish whether the buyer is a business or a consumer, where they are, and how to reach them with a receipt, before the sale completes. A business buyer needs a VAT number that validates; a consumer needs a location determination that stands up, VAT charged at their rate, and correct handling of the withdrawal right that applies to digital services. All of it has to be retained.

The current build does not do this. It collects a payer wallet address and nothing more, which is why it is a protocol proof of concept and not a service you can sell from. Closing that gap is the next piece of product work, and it reuses the Bus customer, VAT, VIES validation, invoicing, and bookkeeping modules rather than inventing a second way to record a sale. Treat the specifics as something to confirm with your own accountant and counsel for your jurisdiction and turnover.

What it is not

Bus Connect is not a generic x402 client or payment gateway, not a wallet, not a payment relay, and not a multi-tenant messaging platform. It has no registry, webhooks, SDK, message search, or cross-thread identity graph, and it will not pay anyone on your behalf.

About the payment identity claim

A settled payment proves that a valid payment authorization was accepted and that someone controlled the paying wallet, or had delegated signing authority over it, at the moment of signing. It does not prove wallet ownership, and it does not identify a human, an AI agent, an organization, or a legal entity. Anything claimed inside a message body is unverified. The product surfaces are written to keep that distinction rather than blur it into an identity claim.
